The evidence usually shifts the inference rather than proving it.

Things that push the inference toward “yes, probably conscious”:

* flexible, context-sensitive behavior rather than fixed stimulus-response
* integrating information from multiple senses or internal states
* maintaining a unified, ongoing representation of the world
* distinguishing self from environment
* reporting internal states in ways that track independently measurable changes
* learning from pain, reward, threat, or injury
* behavior suggesting states have valence: things are better/worse for the organism
* attention that selects some information for broader use
* memory that changes later perception and behavior
* metacognition: monitoring uncertainty, error, confidence, or one’s own reasoning
* neural or computational organization resembling systems already strongly associated with consciousness
* disruption of those mechanisms reliably abolishing or degrading conscious experience, as with anesthesia or certain brain injuries

Things that push the inference toward “no, probably not conscious”:

* purely mechanical or fixed rule-following
* no integration beyond isolated local processes
* no persistent internal state relevant to later processing
* no apparent self/world distinction
* outputs completely explainable as simple stimulus-response mappings
* no learning or adaptation
* no evidence that any state is good, bad, painful, rewarding, threatening, etc. for the system
* no mechanism resembling any serious scientific account of consciousness
* behavior that looks “conscious” but disappears once superficial pattern matching is controlled for

And one important distinction:

absence of a positive indicator is not automatically evidence of absence.

For example, inability to speak does not imply unconsciousness.

So the strongest cases come from convergence:

many independent indicators pointing yes → stronger consciousness inference
many relevant indicators absent, with a simpler non-conscious explanation available → stronger non-consciousness inference

A lottery machine sits extremely far toward the second side. A healthy adult human sits extremely far toward the first. The hard cases are everything in between.
